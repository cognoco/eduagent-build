# Managed Mastery Companion — Codebase-to-Revenue Strategy

> **Exploratory strategy note — 19 July 2026.** This is a future option, not MentoMate canon, roadmap, or an approved build. It asks what business should be created if this codebase had to generate a living for its operator.

## Decision

Do not strip the codebase down to generic infrastructure and search for an unrelated consumer product. Change the buyer and business model while retaining the difficult, differentiated learning machinery.

The recommended first business is a managed practice-and-mastery companion for specialist training providers:

> **We turn an existing high-value training program into a private AI practice and mastery companion, without replacing the provider's current LMS.**

The business should begin as a productized service. Paid customer implementations reveal which repeated elements deserve to become a self-service product later.

## Why this direction

Authentication, security, billing, durable workflows, notifications, auditability, and AI routing reduce the cost of building a product. They do not create customer demand by themselves. Relay, the life-administration concept previously considered for this codebase, demonstrated that distinction: a strong technical fit does not repair weak recurrence, distribution, or willingness to pay.

The codebase's more valuable assets are domain capabilities that are materially harder to reproduce:

- source-grounded conversational teaching;
- adaptive instruction and human override;
- voice interaction;
- concept-level assessment;
- Challenge-based application and explanation;
- spaced repetition and delayed recall;
- evidence of mastery and misconceptions;
- progress, reminders, and durable learner context;
- scoped multi-person visibility and support.

The commercial opportunity is therefore not "another AI assistant." It is to help an established educator prove that learners can understand, recall, explain, and apply what they were taught.

## Initial customer

Target small specialist training companies, certification providers, and product academies that already have:

- trusted proprietary learning material;
- an existing audience and paying learners;
- programs expensive enough that completion and outcomes matter;
- instructors repeatedly answering similar questions;
- a commercial need to demonstrate understanding rather than attendance alone;
- enough control over their material to authorize an AI practice companion.

Avoid broad corporate learning departments initially. They bring long procurement cycles, enterprise integrations, feature expectations, and direct competition with mature learning platforms. Also avoid low-priced course creators whose economics cannot support implementation work.

## Product promise

The provider keeps its existing LMS, videos, live teaching, community, and commercial relationship. The companion begins where content delivery normally ends.

Learners receive:

- short source-grounded conversations about the active material;
- voice or text practice;
- scenarios that require application rather than recognition;
- challenges that ask them to explain, compare, defend, or solve;
- scheduled recall after the original lesson;
- transparent correction and links back to supporting material;
- a clear view of what is understood, weak, or due for review.

Providers receive:

- cohort-level misconception patterns;
- concept-level mastery and retention evidence;
- visibility into where material is unclear;
- signals for targeted instructor intervention;
- an outcome report they can use to improve the program and demonstrate value.

The positioning is:

> **Your LMS delivers the material. The companion makes learners practise it until they can explain and apply it.**

## Why not a generic AI tutor or LMS

The horizontal market is already crowded. Thinkific and Kajabi offer teaching assistants grounded in a creator's course content. Disco provides branded AI learning academies. Trainual, WorkRamp, and Spekit combine AI content generation, knowledge access, quizzes, assignments, reporting, mobile delivery, and workflow integrations.

The new offer must therefore not compete on course hosting, content generation, generic question answering, or administrative breadth. It should compete on deeper guided practice, delayed verification, source provenance, and useful evidence about what learners can actually do.

## Commercial model

Begin with one program and one cohort per implementation. The provider supplies the source material. The service configures the concept structure, teaching behavior, practice scenarios, review timing, and reporting.

An initial engagement could include:

- a six- to eight-week pilot;
- human-reviewed source material, prompts, and challenges;
- learner onboarding for one cohort;
- regular correction and usage review;
- a final mastery, misconception, and retention report;
- a recommendation about continued deployment.

Pricing hypotheses to test in actual sales:

- **Implementation and pilot:** EUR 7,500-15,000;
- **Ongoing service:** EUR 1,000-3,000 per month, depending on learner volume and support;
- **Later product model:** a platform minimum plus a charge per active learner.

These are discovery ranges, not product commitments. The goal is to learn whether a small number of customers can finance the business before engineering a self-service platform.

## Minimum product work

Before the first signed customer, build almost nothing. Produce one excellent demonstration using synthetic or properly licensed material and sell the outcome.

After a customer signs, implement only what the pilot requires:

1. Neutral branding and terminology.
2. A minimal organization and cohort model.
3. Curated source-material import.
4. Learner invitations and access.
5. A minimal instructor view.
6. Program-specific teaching configuration.
7. An exportable outcome report.

Preserve the existing session engine, Challenge mechanics, retention scheduling, source provenance, voice, notifications, LLM routing, profile isolation, and security controls.

Do not initially build:

- a general LMS or course-authoring suite;
- video hosting;
- communities;
- HR or payroll integrations;
- compliance management;
- certificates and credential infrastructure;
- a marketplace;
- elaborate white-label application fleets;
- self-service onboarding for hypothetical customers.

## Ninety-day execution plan

### Days 1-10 — Demonstrate

- Select one specialist body of knowledge.
- Configure a polished learner journey using the existing engine.
- Show the contrast between passive content consumption and active mastery practice.
- Prepare a simple commercial offer with a defined pilot outcome.

### Days 11-30 — Sell

- Identify approximately 50 carefully selected training businesses.
- Contact owners or learning-program leaders directly.
- Demonstrate using a small sample of the prospect's material when permission is available.
- Ask for a paid pilot rather than general product feedback.

### Days 31-60 — Deliver

- Close and deliver the first paid pilot.
- Keep content onboarding and configuration partly manual.
- Observe repeat practice, corrections, instructor use, and learner friction.
- Record which work is genuinely repeated and which is customer-specific.

### Days 61-90 — Repeat

- Close a second customer.
- Standardize only the components repeated across both implementations.
- Decide whether the business is becoming a repeatable service, a narrow SaaS product, or neither.

## Continuation gates

Continue only if the evidence is commercial and behavioral:

1. At least one customer pays meaningful money for the pilot.
2. Learners return for repeated practice rather than asking one-off questions.
3. Providers use the misconception or mastery evidence to make decisions.
4. The companion improves an outcome the provider cares about, such as completion, learner confidence, instructor workload, or demonstrated application.
5. Delivery does not require continuous bespoke prompt repair.
6. A second customer wants substantially the same core service.

If no paid pilot emerges after roughly 30 qualified sales conversations, stop. Do not spend another year polishing the platform in anticipation of demand.

## Product thesis

The fastest path to income is not extracting a speculative new consumer product from the infrastructure. It is using the existing learning engine as a proprietary delivery advantage inside a service business.

Customer work should fund product discovery. The future SaaS, if one exists, should be extracted from repeated paid implementations, not designed in advance from technical possibility.
